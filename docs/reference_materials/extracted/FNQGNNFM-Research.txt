Abstract
Quantum graph neural networks (QGNNs) provide a solution to combine graph representation with Quantum computations in the application area of Molecular Property Prediction (MPP). The challenges in this area are measuring the impact of quantum noise  on training the model, accessing the complexity of the model and regularization. The objective of the present work is to introduce the noise through data (Flipped Labels) and the Quantum hardware. It will stop the model from memorizing the data and acts as regularization towards improving the testing performance of the model.  The present research work, attempt to measure the impact of depolarizing noise using the QM9 dataset. In which HOMO-LUMO is considered as target property. The Quantum circuits are constructed to measure the impact of depolarized noise rating from 0 to 0.20.  The performance of the QGNN is evaluated using training and test mean squared error (MSE). In addition to that generalization gap , fixing the gradient through Root Mean Square (RMS) and measuring the effective complexity of the model. The experimental results indicate the increase in , also show slight increase in training and testing error. The  decreases by 7%, this have no relevance in improving the test prediction accuracy. Due to introducing range of noise  at Quantum circuit level, the effective complexity measure shows major decline along with suppression of output sensitivity. The results demonstrate the noise dependent learning dynamics in Quantum Machine Learning around MPP.

Quantum devices and quantum circuit simulations are affected by imperfect operations, decoherence, and measurement-related limitations. Depolarizing noise provides a useful mathematical model for investigating how such imperfections influence quantum learning. Although noise is often treated as an undesirable source of computational error, its interaction with model complexity and generalization raises an important question: can a controlled amount of noise reduce unnecessary model flexibility, or does it suppress the model's ability to learn meaningful patterns? In conventional machine learning, regularization can improve generalization by limiting overfitting. In quantum machine learning, however, noise may also reduce parameter sensitivity and weaken the gradients required for effective optimization. Consequently, a reduction in the training–test performance gap cannot, by itself, establish that noise provides beneficial regularization.

Molecular datasets such as QM9 offer a suitable experimental setting for investigating these effects. QM9 contains molecular structures and quantum-chemical properties that support supervised molecular prediction tasks. In this study, the HOMO–LUMO energy gap is considered as the target property. By varying the depolarizing noise rate and measuring prediction error, gradient sensitivity, and empirical effective complexity, the study examines how noise influences the learning behavior of a quantum graph model. These measurements provide complementary perspectives: prediction error evaluates the quality of molecular-property estimates, gradient norms characterize local output sensitivity, and effective complexity estimates the extent of locally distinguishable prediction variations under the chosen mathematical definition.

The preliminary experimental results show that increasing depolarizing noise is associated with a decline in training and test performance, a substantial reduction in the root-mean-square output-gradient norm, and a sharp decrease in the measured effective-complexity quantity. Although the generalization gap becomes smaller, this change is not accompanied by improved test accuracy. These observations are therefore consistent with noise-associated suppression of model sensitivity and effective complexity rather than demonstrated beneficial regularization. Nevertheless, the results do not yet establish a causal mechanism or a formal barren plateau; further statistical and numerical validation is required.

The objective of this work is to characterize the relationship between finite depolarizing noise, trainability, empirical effective complexity, and molecular prediction performance in a QGNN-based learning setting. The study aims to distinguish useful complexity reduction from harmful capacity suppression and to identify the experimental conditions under which these behaviors occur. This investigation provides a basis for systematic evaluation across random initializations, training-set sizes, circuit depths, and suitable classical baselines, contributing to a more rigorous understanding of noise-dependent learning behavior in quantum graph models for molecular property prediction.

Conclusion and Future Work: 
The present study investigated the effect of finite depolarizing noise on the prediction performance, trainability, and empirical effective complexity of a quantum graph neural network (QGNN) for molecular property prediction using the QM9 dataset. The experimental results reveal a consistent trend in which increasing noise is associated with reduced output-gradient magnitude, a substantial decline in the measured effective-complexity value, and a slight increase in both training and test errors. The training and test error analysis showed that the lowest observed test mean squared error occurred in the noiseless configuration. Although the generalization gap decreased as the noise rate increased, this reduction did not correspond to improved test prediction accuracy. Instead, the simultaneous increase in training and test errors suggests that the smaller gap may be associated with reduced fitting ability rather than beneficial regularization. The gradient analysis further demonstrated that the RMS output-gradient norm decreased by approximately three orders of magnitude across the investigated noise range. This indicates that the model's output became substantially less sensitive to changes in its trainable parameters. Similarly, the empirical effective-complexity measurement exhibited a sharp decline as noise increased, suggesting a possible reduction in the model's locally measurable predictive flexibility. The precise magnitude of this reduction requires verification of the complexity calculation and its numerical stability.
